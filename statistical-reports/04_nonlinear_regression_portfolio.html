Dataset Overview & Missing Data

The wage dataset (adapted from the ISLR package) contains 3,000 observations and 11 variables describing worker characteristics and wages in the mid-Atlantic region. The target variable is wage (weekly wage in thousands of dollars), and the analysis focuses on predicting it from age, health_ins, jobclass, education, and race.

library(visdat)

vis_dat(wage)

About 1.1% of total data is missing. The maritl column (9% missing) and education column (3% missing) are the affected predictors. Since the proportion of missing data is small, the preprocessing pipeline uses step_naomit(all_predictors()) to remove incomplete cases—a practical choice that avoids introducing bias from imputation while losing very few observations.

Natural Splines for Age: Theory & Setup

Natural splines are piecewise polynomials joined at knots, constrained to be linear beyond the boundary knots. This makes them more stable than unconstrained polynomials at the extremes of the data. The degrees of freedom (df) parameter controls flexibility: a natural spline with df = k has k−1 internal knots placed at quantiles of the predictor.

Example: df = 3 → 2 internal knots at the 33.3rd and 66.7th percentiles of age. For this dataset, the knots fall at age = 37 and age = 48.

Rather than pre-specifying the degrees of freedom, the optimal df was tuned using 10-fold cross-validation with tune():

wage_recipe <- recipe(wage ~ age + health_ins + jobclass + education + race,
                      data = wage) |>
  step_naomit(all_predictors()) |>
  step_ns(age, deg_free = tune())

wf <- workflow() |>
  add_recipe(wage_recipe) |>
  add_model(linear_reg(engine = 'lm'))
grid <- data.frame(deg_free = 1:10)
set.seed(142)
wage_folds <- vfold_cv(wage, v = 10)

tuned_results <- tune_grid(wf, resamples = wage_folds, grid = grid)
show_best(tuned_results, metric = "rmse")

The optimal degrees of freedom for the natural spline is df = 6 (CV RMSE = 34.46). This means 5 internal knots are placed at evenly spaced quantiles of age.

Partial Dependence Profile: Natural Spline

After fitting the finalized spline model on the full dataset, a Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) was generated using the DALEXtra package. The PDP shows the marginal effect of age on predicted wage, averaging over all other predictor values.

model_explainer <- explain_tidymodels(final_fit,
                                      data = wage,
                                      y = wage$wage,
                                      label = 'Linear Regression with Age Spline')

age_profile_pdp <- model_profile(model_explainer, variables = 'age')
ggplot_pdp(age_profile_pdp, age)

The PDP reveals a non-linear, inverted-U relationship between age and wage: predicted wages rise steeply from early career (~age 18) through middle age (~age 40), plateau until approximately age 60, then gradually decline for older workers.

Polynomial Regression for Age: Comparison

As an alternative to splines, a polynomial basis expansion of age was fit with degree tuned via cross-validation over degrees 1 through 10.

wage_recipe_poly <- recipe(wage ~ age + health_ins + jobclass + education + race,
                           data = wage) |>
  step_naomit(all_predictors()) |>
  step_poly(age, degree = tune())

tuned_poly_results <- tune_grid(wf_poly, resamples = wage_folds, grid = grid)
show_best(tuned_poly_results, metric = 'rmse')

High-degree polynomials can produce Runge's phenomenon at extremes

The natural spline is preferred for prediction: its CV RMSE is marginally lower, and its boundary behavior is more stable. High-degree polynomials can exhibit Runge's phenomenon—extreme oscillations at the edges of the data range—which reduces generalization to new observations at unusual age values.

Elastic Net with Natural Spline Features

Finally, the optimal natural spline features were combined with an elastic net penalty to test whether regularization could improve the spline model. A joint grid over penalty λ and mixture α was tuned via 10-fold CV.

wage_recipe_reg <- recipe(wage ~ age + health_ins + jobclass + education + race,
                          data = wage) |>
  step_naomit(all_predictors()) |>
  step_ns(age, deg_free = 6) |>
  step_dummy(all_nominal_predictors()) |>
  step_normalize(all_numeric_predictors())

grid <- expand_grid(penalty = seq(0, 3, by = 0.5),
                    mixture = seq(0, 1, by = 0.1))
# Best parameters: penalty = 0, mixture ≈ 0.9 → CV RMSE = 34.46 # As penalty increases, RMSE consistently rises across all α values.

The best regularized model achieves its minimum RMSE at λ = 0 (no regularization), confirming that penalizing the spline coefficients does not improve prediction in this case. Among non-zero penalties, lasso (α ≈ 0.9) performs marginally better than ridge or elastic net, but all regularized variants trail the unpenalized spline.

Summary & Conclusions

This analysis demonstrates the practical advantages of non-linear modeling when predictors have curved relationships with the outcome. A natural spline with 6 degrees of freedom captured the non-linear age-wage relationship far better than a linear fit, while remaining more numerically stable than a comparable high-degree polynomial.

The partial dependence plots confirm the economic intuition: wages rise through early career, plateau in middle age, and decline in later years—a pattern that a simple linear model would completely miss.

The elastic net analysis further confirms that the spline model is already well-specified: regularization provides no additional benefit, suggesting the predictors are informative and the model is neither overfit nor underfit at the chosen flexibility.
